Ontology manifesto · Part II

The Ontological Rosetta Stone, Part II

A manifesto for Bast AI

Beth Rudden · 26 November 2026

Part II of an evolving manifesto. Read the opening, The Ontological Rosetta Stone.

Nathan Fielder pointed cameras at Elizabeth Holmes for three years. The film is called You Can See Everything, and you can: the kitchen, the kids, the last month before prison.

What you cannot see, in any frame, is what she thinks.

The AI industry is making the camera’s bet at the scale of a civilization. Record enough behavior and the mind will fall out. It won’t. Here is what to build instead.

The Killing Jar

Watch a taxonomist reach for a living domain. Dave Snowden, who created the Cynefin framework, described the scene in 2010. Ontology, he wrote, had come for many to mean a classification system: a set of categories you can assemble into processes so you can pretend the messy and confusing coherence of reality is somehow other, and to be avoided. A complex system, he said, “can only be understood by interacting with it, not by coming along with your butterfly net, a killing charge and a classification system only available to the IT elite.” [1]

He has been saying for fifteen years that taxonomy rhymes with taxidermy. In 2011 he added the mechanism: once a taxonomy is established, if something does not fit, “it will be made to fit.” [2] This May, watching the AI industry turn ontology into a line item, he finished the thought. Ontology “is not a structured vocabulary document. It is not a pipeline. It is not a deliverable.” And then: “The resemblance is not purely phonetic. Both preserve the appearance of life by removing everything that made it alive.” [3]

That is the whole danger in one image. An ontology that is only a schema you deploy is a mounted head on a wall: the shape of the animal, never to move again.

Museums keep the proof. The lion at Gripsholm Castle in Sweden was mounted by a taxidermist who had never seen a lion. Look it up; the face tells you everything. The so-called Magdeburg unicorn was assembled from fossil bones in 1663 by people whose priors already held a unicorn. Put the two side by side and you are looking at a large language model in two specimens. One was built from descriptions, never observation. The other was built from priors, never evidence.

The word deserved better. In 1606 a German scholar named Jacob Lorhard coined ontologia: the account of being. [7] Aristotle made the inquiry the apex of philosophy; Heidegger made it about Dasein, our consciousness of our place in the world. The word survived Kant, the positivists, and the Semantic Web’s reduction of it to an XML vocabulary, because the question it asks never stops mattering: what is actually there, and how do the things that are there relate to each other?

So there are two ontologies in circulation. One asks what exists and how it hangs together: the animal still breathing, still arguing back, still rearranging itself faster than your categories. The other asks what we can label and sort. A catalog is a record of what we killed so we could hold it still. A living ontology is a record of what we are still learning. Most knowledge systems fail in the distance between them, and complexity science was built to measure exactly that distance.

Complexity Demands Structure That Bends

Ontology stopped being a tidy catalog the moment we took complexity seriously. Complexity says the world is made of entanglements: overlapping levels, shifting constraints, and patterns that show up when many parts move together. An honest ontology models those entanglements. It cares about how situations unfold, what they afford, and how they feel from the inside.

Snowden’s own work names the three things any such structure has to hold. The goal of Cynefin, as he puts it, is “maximising the alignment between what things are, how we know things and how we perceive them: ontology, epistemology, and phenomenology.” [4] He is candid that the three “will never fully align.” I read that triad as a Rosetta Stone of its own: three scripts for one situation, and the work is to keep them legible to each other.

William Wimsatt argued for a “tropical rain forest” ontology, rich and interdependent, against Quine’s taste for a “desert” stripped to logical essentials. His test for what is real is robustness: something is real if you can reach it through multiple independent means. A clinical concept confirmed by three practitioners describing it in three different registers. [6]

That is the Rosetta Stone idea as philosophy of science. The original stone worked because the same decree was reachable through three independent scripts: hieroglyphic, demotic, Greek. The stone didn’t unify the scripts. It made them mutually legible. Mutual legibility is a property of language before it is ever a property of systems, which is why language belongs at the center of this work.

Language at the Center

Language is how humans compress complexity. We talk about pain and hope, failure modes and workarounds, values and risks. If our ontologies don’t sit in the loop with language, they calcify into sterile schemas. If our language isn’t grounded in ontologies, it becomes free-floating sentiment. The work is to keep aligning the two: let ontologies evolve through conversation, and let conversations get sharper because they rest on shared structure.

Context is everything, and context requires explicit structure. An American police officer works for local government. A British one belongs to a regional force answerable to national law. The word “boot” is footwear or a car trunk depending on which frame you’re standing inside. These are different maps of reality encoded in the same syllables, and statistical proximity will not sort them out. The ontology doesn’t resolve the ambiguity. It makes the ambiguity visible and navigable.

When GPT arrived in November 2022, information architects were validated and disgusted in the same breath. Validated because language processing finally had a generation engine. Disgusted because the models didn’t need the labeled, hierarchically organized knowledge we had spent decades building. They brute-forced statistical proximity, harvested off the internet without provenance and almost always without consent, and called it understanding. What we had, and the models lacked, was the fuel: semantics, linguistics, knowledge graphs. What the models gave us was the ignition. Nobody has diagnosed the gap between ignition and understanding more precisely, or prescribed for it more wrongly, than Judea Pearl.

Prediction Is Not Understanding

Pearl says no amount of scaling gets us to general intelligence. The limit is mathematical, not computational; you can’t brute-force past a structural gap. He’s right about the disease. LLMs don’t learn how the world works; they learn how humans describe how the world works. They read articles about disease. They never observe disease. Models training on models, averages of averages, every layer carrying the biases of the layer below. [22]

His fix is causality: the do-calculus, counterfactuals, intervention modeling. “What happens if I change X” instead of “what usually follows X.” This is where I get off the bus. Causal inference is still prediction: more sophisticated, structurally constrained, aware of interventions, and still prediction. Pearl added directed edges to the probability graph and called it understanding. Who drew the graph? A human, from a mental model, which is an interpretation. His causal AI inherits exactly the layers of assumption he diagnosed as the disease. The fix for “models of models” can’t be another model. [23]

I have watched a graph inherit a drop-down. Years ago a team of researchers built a “dynamic automation” system, trained it on billions of support tickets, and announced that half of all problems were access issues. I got on a plane and sat with the agents who wrote those tickets. Three screens. Swivel-chair data entry from one system into another. A drop-down of about a hundred codes, and a clock. “Access issue” starts with A. It sat at the top of the list. The model had measured the alphabet and reported it as reality.

The real gap is between prediction and understanding: between a system that tells you what will happen and a system that helps you see what you know. Snowden has drawn this boundary for decades. The do-calculus assumes you can draw the causal graph before you observe the outcome, which holds in a complicated system and fails in a complex one. Healthcare is complex. Markets are complex. Organizations are complex. The graph you drew yesterday describes a system that no longer exists. Ontology doesn’t predict what will emerge; it hands you a vocabulary precise enough to name what’s emerging while it’s happening, and trace where it came from.

Carissa Véliz tells the backstory of this insanity in Prophecy. Statistics, she argues, were built largely by bureaucrats and gamblers, not scientists. The normal curve arrived by two roads, dice and the measurement errors of astronomers, and then Quetelet and Galton turned correlation from a second-best into an explanation in itself. Her sharpest story comes from France. Before the state counted occupations, a man could be part carpenter, part blacksmith. Once the categories carried subsidies, the carpenter became a carpenter, and the statistics improved, at the cost of creating the reality they claimed to describe. If something does not fit, it will be made to fit. “Predictions,” she writes, “are often power moves disguised as quests for knowledge.” [37]

I call this moment apocalyptic in the oldest sense of the word: an unveiling. It has revealed how asinine it has been to let this virus, prediction dressed as fact, take hold in our day and age, starting with investments in magical thinking and something in the water in Silicon Valley. Elizabeth Holmes sold the most famous prediction of the decade: a full lab in a drop of blood.

Which raises the harder question. If the drop-down shaped the tickets, what does the watching shape?

The Tower That Sees Everything

You Can See Everything, from Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim, begins thirty-four days before Elizabeth Holmes reported to prison in 2023. Fielder moved in with Holmes, her partner Billy Evans, and their two children. After she went in, he kept filming the family and stayed in touch with her through 2025. [24] Critics have run out of superlatives, and audiences made it the highest-rated feature in Letterboxd history in its opening weekend. [25] The Rotten Tomatoes critics’ consensus calls it “an inscrutable Rorschach Test of Elizabeth Holmes’ psyche.” [26]

Three years of cameras, and it is still a Rorschach test. In the trailer, Fielder asks where things went wrong. Holmes answers: “I wish I knew. I don’t know. I don’t feel like I understand that.” [24] Even the person inside can’t read the file.

Here is the lesson every AI roadmap should carry: no matter how much data someone has on you, they don’t know what you are thinking. Data records behavior. It never records thought. Snowden made the same point this spring through Polanyi: we can know more than we can tell, and “the assumption that sufficient data and sufficient processing power will eventually capture all knowledge worth having is the Nonaka error in its most extreme form.” [5]

And the watching changes the watched. I have said for years that people change their behavior when they know they are being observed. Physics has its own version of this, which is where we get the phrase “observer effect.” To see an electron you have to bounce light off it, and the light that lets you see it also moves it. Measurement is an interaction; there is no view from nowhere. [27] People are harder. The physicist at least knows she pushed the electron and can account for the push. When a person knows she is watched, she changes, and the watcher has no way to tell which part of the behavior changed. Everything Fielder recorded, Holmes knew he was recording.

That is the fallacy of the surveillance state. It requires people to believe and act outside what they value, and then it mistakes the performance for the person. Stack it up and you get “models of models” at the scale of a society: sensors read behavior, models read the sensors, a second model reads the first, and someone at the top of the tower calls it knowing the population. A tower that sees everything at once sees mostly itself.

The tower also chooses whom to doubt. Holmes was believed for years: by her board, her investors, and the press. A Black woman founder walking into those same rooms with the same pitch and the same empty box would have been asked for the data in the first meeting, and the second, and every one after that. Black founders still receive a fraction of one percent of US venture dollars. [28] Scrutiny lands on the people a system already doubts, and belief flows to the people who look like its last success. My father had a line for this: prejudice is an emotional commitment to ignorance. A model trained on who got believed will inherit that commitment and call it a prior.

This is why cognitive liberty belongs in the architecture and not in the privacy policy. Nita Farahany frames it as self-determination over our own brains and mental experiences. [29] For the systems I build it comes down to two rights. The first is the right not to be inferred: a model’s guess about what you think, want, or will do is a guess, and it should never be stored or treated as a fact about you. The second is the right to read and contest the ontology that labels you. If a system holds a class with your name in it, you get to see that class, see where it came from, and say it is wrong.

Refuse the label. Read the source.

You can only read a source written in a language you share. That brings us back to the stone.

The Rosetta Stone Is a Mapping

The Rosetta Stone is my favorite image for what we’re building. It isn’t a universal grammar; it’s a single artifact where three scripts meet. The power is in the mapping: one text, three representations, and enough alignment to unlock a civilization. An ontological Rosetta Stone is a carefully built correspondence between ways of being and knowing: the conceptual structures of philosophy, the graph structures of computer science, the rubrics of clinical practice or financial regulation or workforce training. It doesn’t have to be global. It has to be good enough that meaning can travel.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy names three uses of ontology in information systems: communication among people, communication between people and machines, and computation. [10] Most systems optimize for the third. The Rosetta Stone serves all three at once. A domain expert opens the ontology and recognizes her knowledge in it. A machine reasons over the axioms to infer what the expert never stated. And the formal structure lets the two negotiate what a concept means. That negotiation is the stone itself.

What the Ontologist and the Data Scientist Should Say

A question went around the ontology community this summer: an ontologist and a data scientist meet. What should they say to each other? I have spent my career as both, and I’ve shipped the answer, so here is mine.

First, name the argument they are walking into. Tom Gruber defined an ontology in 1993 as “an explicit specification of a conceptualization.” [8] This manifesto builds on that definition. Barry Smith and the realist school at Buffalo pushed back hard in “Beyond Concepts” (2004). Ontologies, Smith argued, should represent the universals and particulars that exist in reality, and if an ontology is a mere specification of a conceptualization, “the distinction between good and bad ontologies seems to lose its foothold.” [9] That is a serious objection, and the school that made it built Basic Formal Ontology, now an international standard, and much of the biomedical ontology the life sciences run on. [30]

My position: Smith asks the right question, and provenance answers it. Where the subject is a cell or a skull fracture, reality gets the last word, and a good ontology answers to it. Most of what my customers need to model, though, is the kind of domain Smith himself describes as dealing with “the products of human convention and agreement.” Billing codes. Care pathways. Curricula. Accommodations. Risk. There the conceptualization is part of the reality, and it belongs to the people who live inside it. You keep it honest the way Wimsatt keeps anything honest: several independent routes to each class, and a receipt on every route. Good and bad have a foothold. Can the ontology show where each class came from? Does it survive contact with the data?

So here is what they should say. The data scientist: here is what I measured, how I measured it, and what my measuring changed. The ontologist: here is what I claim exists, and here is how you could prove me wrong. Then both of them: show me the receipt. A model without an ontology can’t tell you what it learned about. An ontology without data can’t tell you whether it’s true. Together they can do what neither does alone, which is to explain.

If you are asking that question, in Buffalo or anywhere else, come argue with me. I’ll bring the receipts.

Women Build Infrastructure

Stones like this do not carve themselves. Someone sits at the edge between two ways of knowing and cuts the correspondence by hand. In the history of computing, the record of who does that cutting has a pattern, and it is worth naming plainly: women build the infrastructure, and men get the monuments.

Grace Hopper insisted that programmers should write in English-like statements and let compilers translate them into machine code. FLOW-MATIC and then COBOL took the ontology of business (records, files, payroll, tax) and rendered it in code. “Subtract income tax from pay” is exactly the move from one conceptual world into another. Hopper built the bridge from machine ontology to business ontology so everyone else could build monuments on top of it. [12]

She also answered the scaling argument before most of today’s model-builders were born. In a 1982 lecture at the NSA, digitized and released by the agency in 2024, she reached for the pioneers: when one ox couldn’t budge the log, “they did not try to grow a bigger ox. They used two oxen.” [13] The industry’s answer to every wall is still a bigger ox. In the same lecture she named where the attention had gone: “we paid no attention to the data. And yet the data is our raw material.” T.S. Eliot asked the underlying question in 1934, in a work titled, conveniently, The Rock: “Where is the wisdom we have lost in knowledge? Where is the knowledge we have lost in information?” [14] That ladder is climbed with structure, not scale. Two oxen pull together only if the yoke fits both, and the yoke is shared structure. That is what an ontology is: the harness that lets separate systems pull in the same direction without anyone having to breed a monster.

Hopper was the visible edge of a workforce. The six women who programmed the ENIAC, Kathleen McNulty, Betty Holberton, Jean Bartik, Marlyn Wescoff, Frances Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman, invented subroutines and debugging strategies working from wiring diagrams, because no programming manuals existed yet. Adele Goldstine wrote the operating manual and trained them. The work was classified as clerical until it became powerful enough to be reclassified as engineering. [15] If something does not fit, it will be made to fit.

The same pattern runs through knowledge management. Heather Hedden wrote The Accidental Taxonomist, and the title admits how women end up holding this work: nobody plans to become the person who structures everyone else’s knowledge. [16] Tara Raafat, at Bloomberg, treats knowledge graphs as the DNA of enterprise information and insists on star-shaped teams, because an ontology built in a silo is an artifact on a shelf. [21] Hedden builds the vocabulary of the discipline; Raafat builds the social structure that keeps it alive. Both are carving stones.

The wall of names is longer, and it should be read like the names cut into a foundation stone. Margie Hlava, whose indexing standards let whole industries find their own documents. Gail Hodge, whose metadata frameworks underpin federal data systems. Maryam Nozari and Michele-Ann Jenkins, who carried semantic architecture into enterprise practice. Enikő Rózsa, co-inventor of ontology-based dialogue systems. Elisa Kendall, who hammered finance standards into ontologies that survived regulators. Jessica Talisman, whose Ontology Pipeline makes the ladder from human curation to machine understanding explicit. Valentina Tamma, who argues knowledge graphs should be the factual backbone of AI. [17–20]

Naming them is not a courtesy. It is provenance, the record of where the work came from. And provenance is older than writing.

Provenance Is How Social Animals Trust

We care about where things come from because we are social animals. To be ostracized was a death sentence in the Paleolithic sense, and the betrayal still lives in the body. So we assess trust constantly: who said this, where did they learn it, and can I trace the chain of custody back to something I can verify myself?

In Rome you can walk this structure with your feet. The Basilica di San Clemente stands on a fourth-century church, which stands on a Roman house and a Mithraeum. The archaeologists didn’t flatten the layers into one floor; they cut stairs between them, so you can descend from the present altar to the pagan shrine and watch meaning change stratum by stratum. Nothing overwritten, every layer reachable, the stairs part of the architecture. I spent my first career reading layers like these. That is what knowledge with provenance looks like.

The W3C PROV-O standard formalizes exactly this structure: entities, the activities that produced them, and the agents responsible. [11] wasDerivedFrom. wasGeneratedBy. wasAttributedTo. The vocabulary reads like a field guide to how humans have judged credibility since before we had writing.

Balzac knew what a fortune without provenance conceals: “The secret of great fortunes without apparent cause is a crime forgotten, for it was properly done.” [33] A model that cannot say where its knowledge came from is a great fortune without apparent cause, the crime forgotten because it was properly done, at scale, in a data center. Bast builds PROV-O in as a structural commitment. Every ontology the system produces carries its lineage: which source document, which reference ontology, which version of the pipeline, and when. When a downstream system makes a clinical recommendation, you can walk the chain all the way back to the source. No black boxes. No trust-me.

When lineage is structural rather than decorative, something falls out of it for free: the explanation.

Explainability Is Infrastructure

Most of the industry treats explainability as something you bolt on after the model ships: a dashboard, a feature-importance plot, a “why” button that produces a plausible sentence nobody can audit. That’s backwards. Explainability is the foundation the rest of the house sits on.

An ontology with provenance doesn’t generate an explanation after the fact. It is the explanation. The reasoning is the structure. When the system extracts a class, you can see which document it came from, which expert pattern shaped it, and which axiom licensed the inference. There’s no separate explainability layer because there’s nothing to reconstruct; the work was visible while it happened, like the stairs at San Clemente. Compare that to a model that produces an answer and a second model that invents a story about why. One of those is infrastructure. The other is public relations.

Shipping this taught me something I didn’t expect: explainable AI is a teaching tool. Our keystone patent describes explanations that adapt to the expertise of the person reading them. In practice, the clinician and the patient get the same truth at different depths. The oncologist sees the class, the axiom, the source document, and the version. The patient sees the same claim in the words she would use at her own kitchen table, with the same source one step away whenever she wants it. Nobody gets a simpler lie. Every good teacher works this way: she meets the student where she stands and walks her down the stairs as far as she wants to go. A black box hands you a verdict and asks for faith. A teacher hands you the reasoning and expects you to check it. The test of an explanation is whether the reader leaves able to ask a better question.

Infrastructure has a tell: it’s expensive, invisible when it works, and catastrophic when it fails. We re-read the expert reference before every generation run. Not cached, not baked into weights: re-read, every time, so the quality bar stays inspectable instead of buried. It’s the plumbing, not the faucet.

Why does the industry keep choosing the faucet? Upton Sinclair named the gravity in 1935: “It is difficult to get a man to understand something, when his salary depends upon his not understanding it.” [32] Treat explainability as a feature and it competes for roadmap space with things that demo better. Treat it as infrastructure and you cannot ship without it. Infrastructure has to be built by something. You need an engine.

An Engine for Stones

Bast is a method for making stones, with no single master ontology at the top. Start from a domain ontology, jump-started from a standard or grown from scratch in a clinic. Run the messy realities of the domain through it: notes, messages, labs, behaviors, emotions. Bind those streams into the graph, then let the graph push back. Where does the ontology fail? Where does lived complexity refuse your categories? Iterate.

The system anchors its output to a curated sample from a 2,233-class medical ontology a clinician built over years of practice. It works as a style guide for good knowledge engineering, not as training data. When the expert gives Abdomen synonyms that run from “Belly” to “Infrabdominal Region,” the system learns that synonym quality means covering the register spectrum. When Skull_Fracture inherits from both Fracture and Head_Wound, it learns that multiple inheritance captures real conceptual overlap. Quality propagates through exemplars. [31]

The hardest step is what we call deep analysis. Standard pipelines extract named entities and map surface relationships: accurate to the text, shallow as knowledge. Deep analysis invents structure the document doesn’t provide. A curriculum for workforce baking training for adults with disabilities never uses the word “Accommodation.” It describes transportation solutions, scheduling flexibility, financial support, and visual aids, all of which are accommodations. The system recognizes the latent grouping and creates the parent class. That’s the difference between extraction and engineering.

Owned, Not Rented

Ontology is suddenly a product category. Palantir has built its platform around an Ontology of objects, links, and actions. Microsoft now ships an ontology inside Fabric IQ so people and agents can share one business vocabulary. [34] These are serious pieces of engineering, and their arrival settles the argument I have been making for years: AI without explicit meaning does not hold.

There is still a line to draw, and every buyer should draw it before signing. Ask three questions. Who owns it? Where did each class come from? Does it live anywhere but here? A living ontology is sourced: every class traces to a document, an expert, or a decision you can read. It is owned: it is the customer’s conceptualization and the customer’s asset, written in open W3C standards like OWL and PROV-O that outlive any vendor. And it is argued with: it changes when the clinic changes. An ontology that makes sense only inside one platform’s runtime is a floor plan of someone else’s building. Useful, and not yours. A catalog can be very sophisticated and still be a catalog.

Bast builds the other kind. The customer owns the stone. We build the engine that carves it, and the body that keeps it alive.

Installing the Heart

Bast takes its name from an Egyptian goddess, a protector with a cat’s head and a long memory. The metaphor was always going to be anatomical. Abdomen, Skull_Fracture: the parts of the body and the ways they fail were the first things the system learned to structure.

So we talk about installing the heart. The heart is the ontological core, the organ that has to be in and beating before anything else circulates. Installing it means the ontology reasoning works reliably enough that meaning pumps through every answer, instead of pooling in some diagnostic corner only an engineer can see. When it’s in, the answer comes back grounded every single time. Not most of the time. Every time. A heart that pumps when it feels like it is a medical emergency, not a feature flag.

A language model with no ontological core is a body on bypass: kept alive by an external machine, statistics circulating in place of meaning. The lights stay on, the answers keep coming, the demo looks alive. Taxidermy looks alive too. Installing the heart means putting a reasoning organ inside the system, so that when you open it up you find structure with provenance, not a pump in a closet. One heart makes one body, and the point was never a single organism.

A Chain of Stones

Over time you don’t get one stone; you get a chain. A stone that links mechanics to anatomy. A stone that links patient narratives to billing codes. A stone that links organizational behavior to patient safety. Each is local and contingent; together they form a path through the complexity.

The chain holds because of provenance. When you step from the patient-narrative stone to the billing-code stone, PROV-O tells you how the mapping was made, by whom, and on what authority. That separates an ontological infrastructure from a pile of crosswalks: every link has a receipt. Which raises the fair question. Where are mine?

The Oracle Learned to Speak Plainly

Prediction has stood this trial before. Nineteen centuries ago Plutarch, a priest at Delphi, wrote a dialogue about why the Pythia no longer answered in verse. His speakers are clear that the god never wrote the lines: “the voice and the phrasing and the metre” belonged to the woman. They quote Heraclitus on the lord whose seat is at Delphi: he “speaks not, nor conceals, but signifies.” And they explain the change. People had come to want answers “clearly and easily with no pomp or artifice,” and they had learned to mistrust “metaphors, riddles, and ambiguities, as so many holes or hiding-places” for a prophet who might trip. The questions of a settled world, whether to marry, whether to sail, whether to lend, called “for a plain, concise answer.” [38] In a companion dialogue on why oracles were falling silent, Plutarch’s speakers call it “foolish and childish in the extreme” to imagine the god climbing inside his prophets like a ventriloquist. [39]

The most trusted prediction engine of the ancient world faced the audit we face now, and it moved toward plain speech. A riddle is a hiding place for the one who made it. Explainability takes the hiding places away.

The Wager

The wager of this manifesto is simple. In a complex world, progress comes less from adding more data and bigger models, and more from building enough shared ontology that we can translate between ways of knowing. You will never see everything. You can see what you know, where you learned it, and who gets to argue with it. The Rosetta Stones I care about are not carved in basalt. They’re built by people, very often women, who sit at the edge between disciplines and make those mappings by hand until the system can help. Bast is my commitment to that work: an engine for stones, not a tower pretending to see everything at once.

Refuse the label. Read the source. Show your work, all the way down.
